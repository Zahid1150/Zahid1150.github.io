Doctoral Research

Healthcare operations · Clemson University · Ongoing

I build optimization models for healthcare workforce scheduling, focused on emergency department physicians and nurses. The models are grounded in evidence from the clinicians themselves: interviews, surveys, work schedules, sleep tracking, and physiological measures.

The goal is schedules that meet staffing requirements while balancing individual preferences, fairness, and fatigue. This work is part of a federally funded project with Prisma Health, Wake Forest University School of Medicine, and the University of Central Florida.

Advisor: Dr. Kevin M. Taaffe, Clemson University.

Optimization models for emergency physician scheduling

Prisma Health's emergency physicians rotate among several emergency department locations. A schedule has to keep every department staffed around the clock and respect credentials, rest rules, and leave, and it shapes recovery opportunities, the distribution of nights and weekends, and alignment with individual preferences. I am working on two connected scheduling problems.

Preference-weighted physician scheduling across multiple EDs

With Dr. Kevin Taaffe and Dr. Hervé Kerivin · Clemson University · in partnership with Prisma Health

One pool of emergency physicians staffs several emergency departments. Each department needs a qualified physician on every day, evening, and night shift, and physicians differ in credentials (general, pediatric, or both), the sites they may work, contract hours, night roles, leave, and day-off requests. I formulate a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model that builds the full month for the whole pool at once.

Each physician weights their own preferences. Every physician spreads 1,000 preference points over 10 categories: shift timing, weekends worked, complete weekends, work blocks and recovery, preferred ED, ED continuity, total work hours, a recurring day off, shift ordering, and night hours. Those points become that physician's own penalty weights in the objective, alongside refused day-off requests and fairness deviations, all measured in points. Because everyone has the same budget, no one can mark everything as top priority.

  • Hard rules: every shift covered by a qualified physician, at most one shift per day with at least 10 hours of rest, contract-hour and night-work bounds, at most six consecutive working days, and site and credential eligibility.
  • Fairness against peers: night hours, weekends, and refused requests are compared with each physician's peer group (role, specialty, and FTE band); only the burden above the group mean, beyond a tolerance, is penalized.
  • Network formulation: the hardest preference, blocks of work followed by enough rest, is modeled as a path through a physician-specific network. Compared with a block-based formulation, this raised the LP bound by 20% (2,860 to 3,420) with 14% fewer binary variables.

Computational study. Fifteen test instances built from a health system's staffing template: 5 EDs, 28 days, 784 shifts (6,804 hours), and a pool of 63, 65, or 67 physicians (shortage, balanced, surplus), each with five random seeds. Hospital names are fictitious and preferences are generated, not yet collected from physicians.

  • Preferences that physicians backed with more points were met more often: 54% for 1–100 points, rising to 89% for more than 300 points. A schedule built from the hard rules alone met 22%.
  • All day-off requests were granted across the 15 instances, and every physician who shares nights received 27 night hours; the spread in night hours per FTE fell from 73 hours (hard rules only) to 12 hours.
  • Mean model-defined satisfaction (the share of a physician's points fully met) was 0.73–0.78 by staffing level, versus 0.22 with the hard rules only.
  • Work blocks and shift timing were the hardest categories to meet, because they compete with coverage, requests, and fair night sharing.
  • Gurobi proved optimality for 20-physician instances; full 65-physician instances reached an 8–12% optimality gap after 23 hours on Clemson's Palmetto cluster. The lower bound is the bottleneck.

Next steps. Branch-and-price decomposition, with physician schedules as columns and the work-block network as the pricing problem, for a stronger bound; preference points collected from physicians; a fatigue-aware objective that trades fatigue against preferences; and multi-month horizons with fairness carried over.

Accepted talk, INFORMS Annual Meeting, November 2026, San Francisco: “Emergency Department Physician Scheduling with Heterogeneous Individual Preferences: A Mixed-Integer Linear Programming Approach” (with K. M. Taaffe and H. Kerivin) · manuscript in preparation.

Fatigue-aware scheduling and operational trade-offs

Physicians in a hospital network rotate across sites, nights, and weekends, and the same shift can tire people differently depending on their body clock. With Dr. Kevin Taaffe and the project team, I built a mixed-integer model in Python and Gurobi for 90-day schedules across four hospitals. It scores each physician–shift pair with a Shift Fatigue Index (chronotype mismatch, shift end time, and shift length) and penalizes uneven sharing of nights, weekends, and distant-site shifts. Themes from the interview study shaped these penalties. Tests use synthetic instances.

  • On a 40-physician instance, a balanced weighting cut total modeled fatigue by 45% (6,893 to 3,777) and the highest individual score by 27%, while other schedule penalties rose 12%.
  • Weighting only fatigue lowered it just 2% more, nearly doubled other penalties, and cut mean recovery after work blocks from 2.9 to 1.7 days.
  • Results held across four randomly generated test instances, with distant-site assignments differing by at most two shifts per physician.
  • The model produced rosters for networks of 40 and 70 physicians; the next step is scaling to larger systems.

The index is a transparent proxy, not yet a validated measure. A 2026 shift study pairs post-shift surveys of fatigue and workload with wrist-worn sleep tracking and before-and-after-shift saliva cortisol; these data will calibrate it.

Presented at the Healthcare Systems Process Improvement Conference (Feb 2026) and the IISE Annual Conference & Expo (May 2026). Next: calibration and faster methods (rolling horizon, decomposition) for larger networks.

Clinician interviews: preferences and fatigue

To ground the models in clinicians' experience, I led a comparative interview study with 18 emergency nurses and 13 attending emergency physicians at Prisma Health. I co-designed the interview guide and conducted semi-structured interviews on Microsoft Teams (April–June 2025). The team analyzed the transcripts in ATLAS.ti with a separate codebook for each profession and checked intercoder agreement on double-coded transcripts. Each clinician described two schedules: the one they would choose overall, and the one best for their fatigue and daily functioning.

  • For 18 of 31 clinicians, the schedule best for fatigue differed from the one they preferred overall: in timing for nine and in structure (shift length, spacing, or blocks) for nine. When timing differed, family obligations, especially childcare, most often pulled the preferred schedule earlier than the hours when clinicians felt they functioned best.
  • Both groups valued recovery, predictability, and long stretches off, and grouped shifts to get them. Nurses linked fatigue to consecutive 12-hour shifts, patient volume, and missed breaks; physicians linked it to night work and rapid switches between shift types.
  • Nurses valued the flexibility of self-scheduling yet still reported fatigue; physicians could state preferences before the schedule was published but had little flexibility afterward.
  • Preference satisfaction and fatigue should be treated as separate objectives rather than one "preference" term. These findings informed the survey questions and the scheduling models.

Schedule alignment, fatigue, and burnout

Burnout is common among emergency department clinicians, and shift scheduling is one factor health systems can change. I helped design and analyzed a cross-sectional survey of 103 nurses and 57 attending physicians at seven Prisma Health emergency departments (March–May 2026). It asked about preferred shift length, consecutive shifts, and night work; how many hours into a shift clinicians begin to feel tired; how much time off they need to recover; and how well their most recent schedule matched their preferences (0–10). I ran the analysis in Python (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels) using group comparisons, mixed models, and robust regression with false discovery rate control.

  • Nurses most often preferred 12-hour shifts (67%); physicians most often preferred 9-hour shifts (49%).
  • 76% preferred shifts longer than the point at which they begin to feel tired (a median of 2 hours longer).
  • Days off needed after five or more consecutive shifts including a night were 2.0 times (nurses) and 2.5 times (physicians) those needed after two to four shifts.
  • Clinicians who rated their schedule a better match to their preferences reported lower burnout (standardized β = −0.40); the association held after also adjusting for sleep, chronotype, and shift type.

These observational results identify an association between schedule fit and burnout and inform the preference-weighted scheduling models above. Next, we are comparing self-reported alignment with the shifts clinicians actually worked before the survey.

Evaluating large language models for thematic analysis of clinician interviews

With S. V. Abrol, V. Prabhu, and K. M. Taaffe · ongoing research

This study examines how well large language models perform inductive thematic analysis of qualitative healthcare interviews, and how their interpretations compare with those of human researchers. Using 18 emergency department nurse interviews and a human-developed reference codebook of 47 codes, we evaluate Claude Sonnet and Opus at medium and high reasoning-effort settings on conceptual alignment, code coverage, coding specificity, and the ability to reproduce higher-order human interpretations.

  • Sonnet captured 33 (medium) and 32 (high) of the 47 human codes; Opus captured 38 (medium) and 39 (high). Raising reasoning effort barely changed coverage, but it changed which concepts were recovered.
  • At least one configuration represented 44 of 47 human codes (93.6%); the best single configuration, Opus at high effort, represented 39 (83.0%).
  • The models produced 10–15 themes against 4 human themes, splitting broad human themes into narrower ones, and their codes often kept the nurse's stated reason (for example, preferring 12-hour shifts to work fewer days). Sonnet kept more narrow, one- or two-nurse codes; Opus more often grouped experiences shared across nurses.
  • No configuration reproduced the human theme on schedule alignment and satisfaction; its pieces appeared scattered across other themes. All four missed three codes, including current-versus-ideal schedule misalignment.
  • LLM-assisted analysis looks most useful for identifying detailed scheduling features, mechanisms, and reasons, while human interpretation remains important for higher-order synthesis.

My contribution: reviewing and refining human–model code mappings, evaluating prompting and analysis strategies, developing the inter-rater reliability assessment, calculating agreement statistics, and contributing to adjudication, interpretation, and the manuscript.

Preliminary findings, not yet peer reviewed · manuscript in preparation.
